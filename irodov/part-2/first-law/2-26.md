# Irodov Problem 2.26 — Internal Energy of Air in a Room

[← Back to 2.2 — The First Law of Thermodynamics. Heat Capacity](/irodov/part-2/first-law.html)

## Problem

Show that the internal energy $$U$$ of the air contained in a room does not depend on its temperature, provided that the atmospheric pressure outside the room remains constant.

Then calculate the internal energy for a room of volume $$40\,\text{m}^3$$ at an atmospheric pressure of $$100\,\text{kPa}$$.

---

## Physical Idea

At first, I was confused because the pressure given in the problem is the **outside atmospheric pressure**, while the internal energy belongs to the air **inside the room**.

The important point is that an ordinary room is not a perfectly sealed container. Air can enter or leave through small openings, doors, ventilation, and other gaps.

Therefore, in equilibrium, the pressure of the air inside the room is approximately equal to the atmospheric pressure outside:

$$
p_{\text{in}}=p_{\text{out}}=p.
$$

Since the outside atmospheric pressure is constant,

$$
p=\text{const}.
$$

The volume of the room is also fixed:

$$
V=\text{const}.
$$

When the temperature increases, some air leaves the room. Therefore, the number of moles decreases while the pressure remains essentially unchanged:

$$
T\uparrow
\quad\Rightarrow\quad
n\downarrow.
$$

---

## My Solution

Air consists mainly of nitrogen and oxygen, so at ordinary temperatures I approximate it as a **diatomic ideal gas**.

For a diatomic ideal gas,

$$
i=5,
$$

where $$i$$ is the number of active degrees of freedom.

The internal energy is therefore

$$
U=\frac{i}{2}nRT.
$$

Substituting $$i=5$$,

$$
U=\frac{5}{2}nRT.
$$

From the ideal gas law,

$$
pV=nRT.
$$

Therefore,

$$
nRT=pV.
$$

Substituting this into the expression for internal energy gives

$$
U=\frac{5}{2}pV.
$$

Since both the atmospheric pressure and the volume of the room are constant,

$$
p=\text{const},
\qquad
V=\text{const},
$$

it follows that

$$
\boxed{U=\text{const}}.
$$

Thus, the internal energy of the air contained in the room is independent of its temperature.

For

$$
p=100\,\text{kPa}=1.00\times10^5\,\text{Pa}
$$

and

$$
V=40\,\text{m}^3,
$$

the internal energy is

$$
U
=
\frac{5}{2}
(1.00\times10^5)
(40).
$$

Therefore,

$$
U=1.00\times10^7\,\text{J}.
$$

---

## Final Answer

$$
\boxed{U=1.0\times10^7\,\text{J}=10\,\text{MJ}}
$$

The internal energy is independent of the room temperature as long as the outside pressure remains constant and the room can exchange air with its surroundings.

---

## What I Learned

At first, I thought that the pressure $$p$$ given in the problem referred only to the air outside the room.

It does refer to the outside atmospheric pressure, but because an ordinary room is not perfectly sealed, the pressure inside remains approximately equal to the pressure outside:

$$
p_{\text{in}}=p_{\text{out}}.
$$

I also initially expected the internal energy to increase when the temperature increased because hotter molecules have greater average kinetic energy.

However, the amount of air inside the room is not constant.

When the temperature increases,

$$
T\uparrow,
$$

some air leaves the room, so

$$
n\downarrow.
$$

Each remaining molecule has more average energy, but there are fewer molecules inside the room. These two effects compensate so that

$$
U=\frac{5}{2}pV
$$

remains constant.

This problem showed me that temperature alone does not determine the **total** internal energy when the amount of gas in the system is allowed to change.
