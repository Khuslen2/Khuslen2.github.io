# Irodov Problem 2.62 — Molecules Remaining in a High Vacuum

[← Back to 2.3 — Kinetic Theory of Gases](/irodov/part-2/kinetic-theory.html)

## Problem

A very high vacuum reaches a pressure of approximately

$$
p=4\times10^{-15}\,\text{atm}.
$$

Assuming the remaining gas is at room temperature, estimate:

1. the number of molecules remaining in $$1\,\text{cm}^3$$ of space;
2. the typical distance between neighboring molecules.

---

## Physical Idea

Even an extremely good vacuum does not necessarily mean that no molecules remain.

At the very small pressure given in the problem, there can still be many molecules in a volume of $$1\,\text{cm}^3$$.

For room temperature, I use the approximate value

$$
T=300\,\text{K}.
$$

First, I can use the ideal gas law to determine the number of moles in $$1\,\text{cm}^3$$.

Then, using Avogadro's constant, I can convert the number of moles into the actual number of molecules.

For the second part, if $$N$$ molecules occupy a total volume $$V$$, the average volume available to one molecule is approximately

$$
\frac{V}{N}.
$$

If the characteristic distance between molecules is $$l$$, I can imagine this volume as a small cube:

$$
l^3\approx\frac{V}{N}.
$$

Therefore,

$$
l\approx\left(\frac{V}{N}\right)^{1/3}.
$$

---

## My Solution

The pressure is

$$
p=4\times10^{-15}\,\text{atm},
$$

and the volume is

$$
V=1\,\text{cm}^3.
$$

Since

$$
1\,\text{cm}^3=1\times10^{-3}\,\text{L},
$$

I use

$$
V=1\times10^{-3}\,\text{L}.
$$

At room temperature,

$$
T\approx300\,\text{K}.
$$

Using the ideal gas law,

$$
pV=nRT.
$$

Therefore,

$$
n=\frac{pV}{RT}.
$$

Using

$$
R=0.08206\,
\text{L}\cdot\text{atm}\,
\text{mol}^{-1}\text{K}^{-1},
$$

I obtain

$$
n=
\frac{
(4\times10^{-15})(1\times10^{-3})
}{
(0.08206)(300)
}.
$$

Thus,

$$
n\approx1.63\times10^{-19}\,\text{mol}.
$$

The number of molecules is

$$
N=nN_A,
$$

where

$$
N_A=6.022\times10^{23}\,\text{mol}^{-1}.
$$

Therefore,

$$
N=
(1.63\times10^{-19})
(6.022\times10^{23}),
$$

which gives approximately

$$
N\approx9.8\times10^4.
$$

Thus, there are roughly

$$
\boxed{N\approx1.0\times10^5\ \text{molecules}}
$$

in every $$1\,\text{cm}^3$$.

### Mean Distance Between Molecules

The average volume available to one molecule is

$$
\frac{V}{N}.
$$

If $$l$$ represents the characteristic intermolecular distance,

$$
l^3\approx\frac{V}{N}.
$$

Therefore,

$$
l\approx
\left(\frac{V}{N}\right)^{1/3}.
$$

Using

$$
V=1\,\text{cm}^3
$$

and

$$
N\approx9.8\times10^4,
$$

I obtain

$$
l\approx
\left(
\frac{1}{9.8\times10^4}
\right)^{1/3}
\text{cm}.
$$

Therefore,

$$
l\approx2.2\times10^{-2}\,\text{cm}.
$$

Since

$$
1\,\text{cm}=10\,\text{mm},
$$

this is

$$
l\approx0.22\,\text{mm}.
$$

Equivalently,

$$
l\approx2.2\times10^{-4}\,\text{m}.
$$

---

## Final Answer

The number of molecules in $$1\,\text{cm}^3$$ is approximately

$$
\boxed{N\approx1.0\times10^5}.
$$

The characteristic distance between neighboring molecules is approximately

$$
\boxed{l\approx2.2\times10^{-4}\,\text{m}\approx0.22\,\text{mm}}.
$$

---

## What I Learned

At first, I thought that such an extremely low pressure would mean that almost no molecules remained in the vacuum.

However, even at

$$
p=4\times10^{-15}\,\text{atm},
$$

there are still approximately $$10^5$$ molecules in every cubic centimeter at room temperature.

I also learned how to move between three different descriptions of a gas:

$$
\text{pressure}
\rightarrow
\text{moles}
\rightarrow
\text{number of molecules}.
$$

The ideal gas law gives the number of moles, while Avogadro's constant converts moles into individual molecules.

Finally, the typical distance between molecules can be estimated from the volume available to each molecule:

$$
l\approx\left(\frac{V}{N}\right)^{1/3}.
$$

This showed me that in a high vacuum the molecules are still present, but they are separated by distances that are enormous compared with ordinary molecular dimensions.
