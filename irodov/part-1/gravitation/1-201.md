# Irodov 1.201 — Jupiter's Orbit

[← Back to Universal Gravitation](/irodov/part-1/gravitation.html)

## Problem

Jupiter's period of revolution around the Sun is approximately 12 times that of the Earth.

Assuming circular planetary orbits, find:

1. how many times the Jupiter–Sun distance exceeds the Earth–Sun distance;
2. Jupiter's orbital velocity and centripetal acceleration in the heliocentric reference frame.

---

## My Approach

Let

- $T_J$ = Jupiter's orbital period,
- $T_E$ = Earth's orbital period,
- $R_J$ = Jupiter–Sun distance,
- $R_E$ = Earth–Sun distance.

We are given

$$
T_J = 12T_E.
$$

---

## (a) Jupiter–Sun Distance

From Kepler's third law,

$$
\frac{T_J^2}{R_J^3}
=
\frac{T_E^2}{R_E^3}.
$$

Rearranging,

$$
\left(\frac{R_J}{R_E}\right)^3
=
\left(\frac{T_J}{T_E}\right)^2.
$$

Therefore,

$$
\frac{R_J}{R_E}
=
\left(\frac{T_J}{T_E}\right)^{2/3}.
$$

Since

$$
\frac{T_J}{T_E}=12,
$$

we obtain

$$
\frac{R_J}{R_E}
=
12^{2/3}
\approx5.24.
$$

Thus,

$$
\boxed{\frac{R_J}{R_E}\approx5.2}.
$$

So Jupiter is approximately **5.2 times farther from the Sun than the Earth is**.

Using

$$
R_E\approx1.50\times10^{11}\,\mathrm{m},
$$

the orbital radius of Jupiter is

$$
R_J
=
12^{2/3}R_E
\approx
7.86\times10^{11}\,\mathrm{m}.
$$

---

## (b) Jupiter's Orbital Velocity

Let $M_J$ be Jupiter's mass and $M_S$ the mass of the Sun.

For Jupiter's circular orbit, the gravitational force provides the required centripetal force:

$$
G\frac{M_JM_S}{R_J^2}
=
\frac{M_Jv_J^2}{R_J}.
$$

The mass of Jupiter cancels:

$$
\frac{GM_S}{R_J}
=
v_J^2.
$$

Therefore,

$$
v_J
=
\sqrt{\frac{GM_S}{R_J}}.
$$

Using

$$
G=6.6743\times10^{-11}
\,\mathrm{m^3\,kg^{-1}\,s^{-2}},
$$

$$
M_S=1.989\times10^{30}\,\mathrm{kg},
$$

and

$$
R_J\approx7.86\times10^{11}\,\mathrm{m},
$$

we obtain

$$
v_J
\approx
1.30\times10^4\,\mathrm{m/s}.
$$

Therefore,

$$
\boxed{v_J\approx1.3\times10^4\,\mathrm{m/s}}
$$

or

$$
\boxed{v_J\approx13\,\mathrm{km/s}}.
$$

---

## Jupiter's Centripetal Acceleration

The centripetal acceleration is

$$
a_J
=
\frac{v_J^2}{R_J}.
$$

Since

$$
v_J^2=\frac{GM_S}{R_J},
$$

we can also write

$$
a_J
=
\frac{GM_S}{R_J^2}.
$$

Using

$$
R_J
=
12^{2/3}R_E,
$$

we have

$$
R_J^2
=
12^{4/3}R_E^2.
$$

Therefore,

$$
a_J
=
\frac{1}{12^{4/3}}
\frac{GM_S}{R_E^2}.
$$

Substituting the numerical values gives approximately

$$
a_J
\approx
2.1\times10^{-4}\,\mathrm{m/s^2}.
$$

Thus,

$$
\boxed{a_J\approx2.1\times10^{-4}\,\mathrm{m/s^2}}.
$$

---

## Final Answer

The Jupiter–Sun distance compared with the Earth–Sun distance is

$$
\boxed{\frac{R_J}{R_E}\approx5.2}.
$$

Jupiter's orbital velocity is

$$
\boxed{v_J\approx13\,\mathrm{km/s}},
$$

and its centripetal acceleration is

$$
\boxed{a_J\approx2.1\times10^{-4}\,\mathrm{m/s^2}}.
$$

---

## What I Learned

This problem connects **Kepler's third law** with **Newton's law of gravitation**.

Kepler's third law first allows the orbital radius of Jupiter to be determined from its period:

$$
T^2\propto R^3.
$$

Once the orbital radius is known, Newton's law of gravitation gives the orbital velocity and centripetal acceleration.

Another useful observation is that Jupiter's mass cancels from the orbital equation. For a circular orbit around the Sun,

$$
v=\sqrt{\frac{GM_S}{R}},
$$

so the orbital speed depends on the Sun's mass and the orbital radius, not on the mass of the orbiting planet.

### Notation Note

In my handwritten solution I initially called $R_E$ "Earth's radius."

More precisely, $R_E$ here means the **radius of Earth's orbit**, or approximately the Earth–Sun distance. It does not mean the physical radius of the Earth.
