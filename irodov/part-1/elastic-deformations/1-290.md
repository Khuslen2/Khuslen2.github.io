# Irodov 1.290 — Thermal Expansion of a Steel Cylinder

[← Back to Elastic Deformations of a Solid Body](/irodov/part-1/elastic-deformations.html)

## Problem

What pressure has to be applied to the ends of a steel cylinder to keep its length constant on raising its temperature by \(100^\circ\mathrm{C}\)?

---

## Physical Idea

When the steel cylinder is heated, it naturally tries to expand.

At the same time, pressure is applied to both ends of the cylinder, producing compression.

The pressure must produce exactly enough compression to cancel the thermal expansion:

$$
\text{thermal expansion}
+
\text{elastic compression}
=
0.
$$

Therefore, the total change in length is

$$
\Delta L_{\mathrm{total}}=0.
$$

---

## My Solution

The temperature increase is

$$
\Delta T=100^\circ\mathrm{C}.
$$

If the cylinder were allowed to expand freely, its thermal elongation would be

$$
\Delta L_{\mathrm{thermal}}
=
\alpha L_0\Delta T.
$$

Dividing by the original length \(L_0\),

$$
\frac{\Delta L_{\mathrm{thermal}}}{L_0}
=
\alpha\Delta T.
$$

Therefore, the thermal strain is

$$
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{thermal}}
=
\alpha\Delta T.
$$

Because the cylinder must keep its original length,

$$
\Delta L_{\mathrm{total}}=0.
$$

This means that the compression caused by the applied pressure must exactly cancel the thermal expansion.

Thus,

$$
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{thermal}}
+
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{compression}}
=
0.
$$

The thermal strain is positive:

$$
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{thermal}}
=
+\alpha\Delta T.
$$

Now consider the compression.

Young's modulus is defined as

$$
E
=
\frac{\text{stress}}{\text{strain}}.
$$

The pressure applied to the ends of the cylinder is the compressive stress:

$$
p=\frac{F}{A}.
$$

Therefore, the magnitude of the compressive strain is

$$
\frac{p}{E}.
$$

Since compression decreases the length, we write

$$
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{compression}}
=
-\frac{p}{E}.
$$

Therefore,

$$
\alpha\Delta T-\frac{p}{E}=0.
$$

Rearranging,

$$
\frac{p}{E}
=
\alpha\Delta T,
$$

so

$$
p
=
E\alpha\Delta T.
$$

For steel,

$$
E
=
2.0\times10^{11}\ \mathrm{Pa},
$$

and

$$
\alpha
=
1.2\times10^{-5}\ \mathrm{K}^{-1}.
$$

Also,

$$
\Delta T
=
100\ \mathrm{K}.
$$

Substituting,

$$
p
=
(2.0\times10^{11})
(1.2\times10^{-5})
(100).
$$

Therefore,

$$
p
=
2.4\times10^8\ \mathrm{Pa}.
$$

Since

$$
1\ \mathrm{MPa}=10^6\ \mathrm{Pa},
$$

we can also write

$$
p=240\ \mathrm{MPa}.
$$

---

## Final Answer

$$
\boxed{p=2.4\times10^8\ \mathrm{Pa}}
$$

or

$$
\boxed{p=240\ \mathrm{MPa}}.
$$

---

## My Mistake

At first, I was confused because the object in the problem is a **steel cylinder**, so I thought I might have to consider its volume.

However, the problem specifically asks us to keep its **length constant**.

Therefore, the important deformation is the change in length, and we use linear thermal expansion:

$$
\Delta L
=
\alpha L_0\Delta T.
$$

We do not need the volume expansion formula.

I also had to recognize that there are two different strains acting at the same time.

Heating produces an elongation:

$$
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{thermal}}
=
+\alpha\Delta T,
$$

while pressure produces a compression:

$$
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{compression}}
=
-\frac{p}{E}.
$$

The phrase **"keep its length constant"** means that these two effects must exactly cancel:

$$
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{thermal}}
+
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{compression}}
=
0.
$$

---

## What I Learned

The key phrase in this problem is **"keep its length constant."**

It does not mean that the steel stops trying to expand when it is heated.

Instead, two effects occur simultaneously:

$$
\text{heating}
\quad\longrightarrow\quad
\text{expansion},
$$

while

$$
\text{applied pressure}
\quad\longrightarrow\quad
\text{compression}.
$$

For the length to remain unchanged, these two strains must cancel:

$$
\boxed{
\alpha\Delta T-\frac{p}{E}=0
}.
$$

I also learned the connection between **stress, strain, and pressure**.

Young's modulus gives

$$
E
=
\frac{\text{stress}}{\text{strain}},
$$

and because the force is applied over the end area of the cylinder,

$$
\text{stress}
=
\frac{F}{A}
=
p.
$$

So the compressive strain produced by pressure has magnitude

$$
\frac{p}{E}.
$$

Another important point is that the problem does not give the length or diameter of the cylinder.

They are not needed.

By working with strain,

$$
\frac{\Delta L}{L_0},
$$

the original length cancels, and by working with stress,

$$
\frac{F}{A},
$$

we directly obtain the pressure.

Therefore, the required pressure depends only on the material properties of steel and the temperature change:

$$
\boxed{
p=E\alpha\Delta T
}.
$$
