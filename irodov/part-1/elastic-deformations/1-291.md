# Irodov 1.291 — Internal Pressure in a Glass Tube and Spherical Flask

[← Back to Elastic Deformations of a Solid Body](/irodov/part-1/elastic-deformations.html)

## Problem

What internal pressure, in the absence of external pressure, can be sustained

**(a)** by a glass tube;

**(b)** by a glass spherical flask,

if in both cases the wall thickness is

$$
\Delta r=1.0\ \mathrm{mm}
$$

and the radius is

$$
r=25\ \mathrm{mm}?
$$

---

## Physical Idea

The internal pressure tries to split the vessel apart.

The glass wall resists this separation through tensile stress.

Therefore, in both parts we use the same basic idea:

$$
\boxed{
F_{\mathrm{pressure}}
=
F_{\mathrm{glass}}
}
$$

The important point is that the pressure force is not found from the entire curved surface area.

Instead, we use the **projected area perpendicular to the direction in which the vessel is trying to separate**.

The tube and the spherical flask have different projected areas and different resisting cross-sectional areas, which is why they can sustain different pressures.

---

## My Solution

### (a) Glass tube

Let the length of the tube be L.

Imagine cutting the tube lengthwise into two equal halves.

The internal pressure tries to separate these two halves.

The projected area of one half of the tube is a rectangle whose dimensions are

$$
2r
$$

and

$$
L.
$$

Therefore,

$$
S_{\mathrm{proj}}
=
2rL.
$$

The separating force produced by the internal pressure is

$$
F_{\mathrm{pressure}}
=
pS_{\mathrm{proj}}.
$$

Thus,

$$
F_{\mathrm{pressure}}
=
p(2rL).
$$

So,

$$
F_{\mathrm{pressure}}
=
2prL.
$$

Now consider the glass wall resisting the separation.

There are two thin strips of glass along the longitudinal cut.

The cross-sectional area of each strip is

$$
\Delta r L.
$$

Therefore, the total resisting cross-sectional area is

$$
S_{\mathrm{glass}}
=
2\Delta r L.
$$

Let the maximum tensile stress of glass be

$$
\sigma.
$$

Then the maximum resisting force of the glass is

$$
F_{\mathrm{glass}}
=
\sigma(2\Delta r L).
$$

At the limiting pressure,

$$
F_{\mathrm{pressure}}
=
F_{\mathrm{glass}}.
$$

Therefore,

$$
p(2rL)
=
\sigma(2\Delta r L).
$$

Cancelling the common factor 2L,

$$
pr
=
\sigma\Delta r.
$$

Hence,

$$
p
=
\sigma\frac{\Delta r}{r}.
$$

For glass, take

$$
\sigma
=
5.0\times10^7\ \mathrm{Pa}.
$$

Using

$$
\Delta r
=
1.0\ \mathrm{mm},
$$

and

$$
r
=
25\ \mathrm{mm},
$$

we obtain

$$
p
=
5.0\times10^7
\frac{1.0}{25}.
$$

Therefore,

$$
\boxed{
p_{\mathrm{tube}}
=
2.0\times10^6\ \mathrm{Pa}
}
$$

or

$$
\boxed{
p_{\mathrm{tube}}
=
2.0\ \mathrm{MPa}
}.
$$

---

### (b) Glass spherical flask

Now imagine cutting the spherical flask through its center into two hemispheres.

The internal pressure tries to separate the two hemispheres.

The projected area of one hemisphere is a circle of radius r.

Therefore,

$$
S_{\mathrm{proj}}
=
\pi r^2.
$$

The force produced by the internal pressure is

$$
F_{\mathrm{pressure}}
=
p\pi r^2.
$$

Now consider the glass resisting the separation.

Along the circular cut, the glass forms a thin ring.

The circumference of the ring is

$$
2\pi r.
$$

Multiplying the circumference by the wall thickness gives the resisting cross-sectional area:

$$
S_{\mathrm{glass}}
=
2\pi r\Delta r.
$$

Therefore,

$$
F_{\mathrm{glass}}
=
\sigma(2\pi r\Delta r).
$$

At the limiting pressure,

$$
F_{\mathrm{pressure}}
=
F_{\mathrm{glass}}.
$$

Thus,

$$
p\pi r^2
=
\sigma(2\pi r\Delta r).
$$

Cancelling the common factor

$$
\pi r,
$$

we obtain

$$
pr
=
2\sigma\Delta r.
$$

Therefore,

$$
p
=
2\sigma\frac{\Delta r}{r}.
$$

Substituting the values,

$$
p
=
2
\left(5.0\times10^7\right)
\frac{1.0}{25}.
$$

Hence,

$$
\boxed{
p_{\mathrm{sphere}}
=
4.0\times10^6\ \mathrm{Pa}
}
$$

or

$$
\boxed{
p_{\mathrm{sphere}}
=
4.0\ \mathrm{MPa}
}.
$$

---

## Final Answer

For the glass tube,

$$
\boxed{
p_{\mathrm{tube}}
=
2.0\ \mathrm{MPa}
}.
$$

For the glass spherical flask,

$$
\boxed{
p_{\mathrm{sphere}}
=
4.0\ \mathrm{MPa}
}.
$$

Therefore, for the same material, radius, and wall thickness, the spherical flask can sustain twice the internal pressure of the cylindrical tube.

---

## My Mistake

At first, I did not know what area should be used to calculate the force produced by the internal pressure.

For the tube, I initially thought about portions of the circular circumference and the curved surface of the tube.

However, the important area is not the curved surface area.

The pressure force that separates the two halves is found using the **projected area perpendicular to the direction of separation**.

For the tube, this projected area is

$$
S_{\mathrm{proj}}
=
2rL.
$$

It is simply a rectangle with width equal to the diameter of the tube and length equal to L.

Another useful check is dimensional analysis.

An expression such as

$$
2\pi L
$$

cannot represent an area because it has the dimensions of length rather than length squared.

For the spherical flask, I used the same physical idea.

When the sphere is divided into two hemispheres, its projected area is not the surface area of a hemisphere.

It is the area of the circular projection:

$$
S_{\mathrm{proj}}
=
\pi r^2.
$$

The main difficulty in this problem was therefore not the algebra, but identifying the correct areas on which the forces act.

---

## What I Learned

The most important idea in this problem is how to convert a distributed pressure into an equivalent force.

Pressure acts over the entire curved inner surface, but when considering whether the vessel will split into two parts, the resultant pressure force can be found from the projected area.

For the cylindrical tube,

$$
S_{\mathrm{proj}}
=
2rL,
$$

which gives

$$
F_{\mathrm{pressure}}
=
2prL.
$$

For the spherical flask,

$$
S_{\mathrm{proj}}
=
\pi r^2,
$$

which gives

$$
F_{\mathrm{pressure}}
=
p\pi r^2.
$$

I also learned that the glass resists the pressure through **tensile stress in the wall**.

The resisting force is

$$
F
=
\sigma S,
$$

where S is the cross-sectional area of the glass carrying the tension.

For the tube,

$$
S_{\mathrm{glass}}
=
2\Delta r L,
$$

while for the sphere,

$$
S_{\mathrm{glass}}
=
2\pi r\Delta r.
$$

This gives two important results:

$$
p_{\mathrm{tube}}
=
\sigma\frac{\Delta r}{r},
$$

and

$$
p_{\mathrm{sphere}}
=
2\sigma\frac{\Delta r}{r}.
$$

Therefore,

$$
\boxed{
p_{\mathrm{sphere}}
=
2p_{\mathrm{tube}}
}.
$$

This explains physically why a spherical pressure vessel is stronger than a cylindrical one when the radius, wall thickness, and material are the same.
