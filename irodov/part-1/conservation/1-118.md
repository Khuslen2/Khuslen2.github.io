# Irodov 1.118 — Work of a Constant Force

[← Back to Conservation Laws](/irodov/part-1/conservation.html)

## Problem

A particle moves in the $xy$-plane from point 1 to point 2.

The initial and final position vectors are

$$
\mathbf r_1=\mathbf i+2\mathbf j,
$$

and

$$
\mathbf r_2=2\mathbf i-3\mathbf j.
$$

During the motion, one of the forces acting on the particle is

$$
\mathbf F=3\mathbf i+4\mathbf j.
$$

Find the work done by this force.

---

## My First Attempt

Since the actual trajectory between the two points was not specified, I first assumed that the particle moved directly from point 1 to point 2 along a straight line.

Using

$$
\mathbf r_1=(1,2),
$$

and

$$
\mathbf r_2=(2,-3),
$$

I found the straight line connecting the two points.

I then tried to calculate the angle between the force and the trajectory and use

$$
W=\int F\cos\theta\,dr.
$$

This gave me a positive value of about

$$
30.9\,\mathrm{J}.
$$

However, this result was not correct.

---

## What Went Wrong

There were two problems with my reasoning.

First, I assumed a straight-line path before proving that the work does not depend on the actual trajectory.

For a general force, that would not be allowed.

In this problem, the trajectory turns out not to matter because the force

$$
\mathbf F=3\mathbf i+4\mathbf j
$$

is a **constant vector**.

Second, in the work integral,

$$
dW=\mathbf F\cdot d\mathbf r,
$$

the vector $d\mathbf r$ is the infinitesimal displacement.

It is not the same thing as the position vector $\mathbf r$.

That distinction is essential.

---

## Correct Solution

The work done by a force is

$$
W=\int_1^2 \mathbf F\cdot d\mathbf r.
$$

Since $\mathbf F$ is constant,

$$
W
=
\mathbf F\cdot\int_1^2 d\mathbf r.
$$

But

$$
\int_1^2 d\mathbf r
=
\mathbf r_2-\mathbf r_1.
$$

Therefore,

$$
W
=
\mathbf F\cdot(\mathbf r_2-\mathbf r_1).
$$

Now calculate the displacement vector:

$$
\mathbf r_2-\mathbf r_1
=
(2\mathbf i-3\mathbf j)
-
(\mathbf i+2\mathbf j).
$$

Thus,

$$
\Delta\mathbf r
=
\mathbf i-5\mathbf j.
$$

Therefore,

$$
W
=
(3\mathbf i+4\mathbf j)
\cdot
(\mathbf i-5\mathbf j).
$$

Using

$$
\mathbf i\cdot\mathbf i=1,
$$

$$
\mathbf j\cdot\mathbf j=1,
$$

and

$$
\mathbf i\cdot\mathbf j=0,
$$

we get

$$
W
=
3(1)+4(-5).
$$

Hence,

$$
W=3-20.
$$

Therefore,

$$
\boxed{W=-17\,\mathrm{J}}
$$

---

## Physical Check

The force has components

$$
F_x=3,
\qquad
F_y=4.
$$

The total displacement is

$$
\Delta x=1,
\qquad
\Delta y=-5.
$$

So the work can also be written as

$$
W=F_x\Delta x+F_y\Delta y.
$$

Therefore,

$$
W=3(1)+4(-5)
=-17\,\mathrm{J}.
$$

The negative sign makes physical sense.

The force points partly upward, while the particle moves strongly downward overall.

The negative contribution from the $y$-direction is larger than the positive contribution from the $x$-direction.

---

## Final Answer

$$
\boxed{W=-17\,\mathrm{J}}
$$

---

## What I Learned

This problem taught me that I should not assume a path is irrelevant unless there is a physical or mathematical reason.

Here, the reason is that the force is constant.

For a constant force,

$$
W
=
\mathbf F\cdot\Delta\mathbf r.
$$

So the work depends only on the initial and final positions.

I also learned to distinguish carefully between

$$
\mathbf r
$$

and

$$
d\mathbf r.
$$

The first is a position vector.

The second is an infinitesimal displacement vector.

Confusing them can completely change the meaning of the work calculation.
